— Inheritance Law

Inheritance Lawyer in Antalya

Inheritance disputes often arise from transfers made by the deceased during their lifetime. Reviewing the land registry records and the dates of transfer determines which action should be brought. Inheritance matters are handled in Antalya under the Turkish Civil Code.

Scope of services

Obtaining the certificate of inheritance, division of the estate, determination of the estate, abatement, title cancellation and registration based on simulated transfers, renunciation of inheritance and annulment of a will are handled.

In disputes between heirs the extent of the estate is established first: immovable property, bank accounts, vehicles and any company shares are identified.

In files with a foreign element, assets located abroad and the effect of foreign court decisions in Türkiye are assessed separately.

Reserved portion and abatement

Although the deceased is free to dispose of their assets, they cannot infringe the reserved portions set by law for descendants, parents and the spouse. An heir whose reserved portion is infringed may bring an action for abatement.

In such an action, gratuitous transfers made during the lifetime of the deceased are assessed together with dispositions upon death. The calculation is based on the value of the estate and generally requires expert examination.

Where the deceased made apparent sales in order to conceal assets from an heir, the transfer may be challenged as a simulated transaction. The action examines whether the real price was paid and the relationship between the parties.

Wills and inheritance agreements

The deceased may determine the fate of their assets by a disposition upon death. A will may be made in official form, in the testator's own handwriting or orally, each with different validity requirements.

A holograph will must be written entirely in the testator's own hand, contain a date and be signed. The absence of any of these may constitute grounds for annulment.

Annulment may be based on lack of capacity, defective intent, non-compliance with form or unlawful content. The action is subject to time limits, and until it is brought the will remains valid.

Renunciation of inheritance

Where the debts of the deceased exceed their assets, renunciation comes into play. The declaration is made to the civil court of peace and is subject to a time limit.

If the insolvency of the deceased was evident or officially established at the date of death, the inheritance is deemed renounced by operation of law, and a declaratory action may be brought.

The consequences are wide-ranging: on renunciation the share passes to the other heirs or to the next generation. The decision should therefore not be taken before the extent of the estate has been examined.

Documents required

The death certificate and civil registry records of the deceased.

The certificate of inheritance, showing the shares.

Land registry records and, where transfers were made, the deeds and dates of transfer.

Bank statements, vehicle records and any company shareholding documents.

Any will or inheritance agreement, in original or certified copy.

Time limits

Renunciation must be made within the statutory period, which as a rule runs from the date the heir learns of the death and of their status as heir.

An action for abatement is subject to periods running from the date the heirs learn that their reserved portions have been infringed. Missing these periods extinguishes the right of action.

In title cancellation actions based on a simulated transfer, limitation does not as a rule apply; there is nevertheless a risk that evidence becomes unavailable over time.

The duration of a division action depends on the number of heirs, the number of properties and expert examination, and cannot be stated in advance.

Common mistakes

Renouncing the inheritance before the extent of the estate has been examined, which may cause loss of rights.

Not investigating transfers made during the lifetime of the deceased, so that abatement and simulation claims are not raised in time.

Failing to raise a claim for occupancy compensation in time where one heir uses the property alone, which limits recovery for past periods.

Using bank accounts after the date of death, which may give rise to liability towards the estate.

A will that does not comply with the required form, which invites an annulment claim.
