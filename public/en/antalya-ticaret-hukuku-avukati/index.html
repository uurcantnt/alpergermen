Commercial Lawyer in Antalya

A large share of commercial disputes stems from details overlooked when the contract was drawn up. How a contract is written determines which party will rely on which evidence once a dispute arises. Advice and litigation are provided in Antalya under the Turkish Commercial Code (Law No. 6102).

Scope of services

Support is provided for company formation, preparation of articles of association, capital increases and reductions, conversions, mergers and demergers.

Commercial contracts are drafted and reviewed, including sale, distribution, franchise, service and supply agreements together with penalty clauses and security arrangements.

Disputes between shareholders, annulment of general assembly resolutions, requests for dissolution on just grounds and actions concerning directors' liability are handled. Enforcement proceedings and litigation are conducted for the collection of commercial receivables.

Companies and shareholder relations

In limited and joint stock companies the rights and obligations of shareholders, the procedure for share transfers and the operation of the general assembly differ. When choosing the company type, the liability regime and the ease of transfer are assessed together.

An action to annul a general assembly resolution may be brought within the statutory period by specific persons. Whether the notice of meeting complied with the required procedure is examined when assessing validity.

Where the relationship of trust between shareholders has broken down and the conditions are met, dissolution on just grounds may be sought. Instead of dissolution, the court may order payment of the real value of the claimant's shares.

Points to watch in commercial contracts

The contract should set out the parties' obligations, delivery and payment terms, the consequences of delay and the grounds on which it ends.

Penalty clauses, security arrangements and default interest provisions directly affect the burden of proof once a dispute arises. Different rules apply to the reduction of penalty clauses in relationships between merchants.

Clauses on competent court and applicable law, and any arbitration clause, determine where and how a dispute will be resolved. These provisions can rarely be changed afterwards.

Commercial books and evidence

In disputes between merchants, commercial books are an important means of proof. Books kept in due form, with opening and closing certifications, may also serve as evidence in favour of their owner.

The books must corroborate one another and the entries must be consistent with the underlying documents. Where the other party's books contain entries against their own interest, those entries count as evidence against them.

Books kept electronically, together with e-invoice and e-waybill records, are also assessed as evidence. Preserving the integrity of these records matters for later disputes.

How the process runs

The documents of the commercial relationship are reviewed: contract, order forms, waybills, invoices and correspondence.

As mediation is a condition of the action for monetary claims, an application is made and the meetings are conducted.

If no agreement is reached, an action is filed before the commercial court of first instance or enforcement proceedings are initiated.

Requests for expert examination, review of commercial books and site inspection are submitted.

Appellate remedies are assessed and collection of the final award is followed up.

Time limits

Limitation periods for commercial receivables vary with the type of contract; the general period differs from the shorter periods specific to certain contract types.

Defective delivery gives rise to duties of inspection and notification. Between merchants, obvious defects must be notified immediately after delivery and hidden defects without delay once discovered.

An action to annul a general assembly resolution must be brought within the statutory period; once it expires the right of action is lost.

Rights of recourse and limitation periods for negotiable instruments are regulated separately and are shorter than for other claims.

Common mistakes

Not putting the contract in writing or not retaining order correspondence, which makes the scope of the obligation difficult to prove.

Failing to give notice of defects in time and in writing, which prevents claims based on defect from being raised.

Not keeping commercial books in due form, which removes their capacity to serve as evidence in favour of their owner.

Accepting jurisdiction and arbitration clauses without reading them, which may lead to the dispute being heard in an unexpected forum.

Overlooking mediation as a condition of the action, which results in dismissal on procedural grounds.
